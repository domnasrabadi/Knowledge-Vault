# Toward an Evaluation Science for Generative AI Systems

## Metadata
- Author: Laura Weidinger, Inioluwa Deborah Raji, Hanna Wallach, Margaret Mitchell, Angelina Wang, Olawale Salaudeen, Rishi Bommasani, Deep Ganguli, Sanmi Koyejo, William Isaac
- URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.05336
## Highlights
* evaluation science = approach that treats the assessment of generative AI systems as a scientific discipline
	* builds theories expressed as testable hypotheses
	* employs measurement instruments with reliability (internal validity) and generalisability (external validity)
	* advances through iterative refinement of methods and insights
* static benchmarks are insufficient
	* lack alignment with the complex, evolving contexts in which AI products are deployed
* key lessons drawn from mature engineering fields
	* target real-world performance, not proxy tasks
	* refine measurement approaches iteratively, updating targets and instruments over time
	* establish processes and institutions that support shared tools, standards, and oversight
* real-world performance = how the system behaves in deployment contexts
	* early pre-deployment testing surfaces risks when mitigation is cheaper
	* post-deployment monitoring captures emergent harms from user interactions
	* many issues arise only through complex, context-specific use
* iterative refinement of metrics
	* select and update measurement targets as understanding deepens
	* triangulate multiple instruments to gain robust insights, since no single metric is perfect
	* recognise interdependence between identifying targets, designing metrics, and building instruments
* open-ended design of generative AI systems
	* supports diverse, underspecified use cases, complicating precise target definitions
	* stochastic outputs mean identical inputs can yield different responses
	* unknown training data factors add further variability
* longitudinal interaction risks
	* prolonged human–AI engagement can create evolving harms such as unhealthy relationships or persuasion dynamics
* moving beyond “general intelligence”
	* adopt task-specific, real-world relevant measures of progress and safety
* decomposing complex risks into subcomponents
	* example: misinformation
		* factual accuracy = whether the output is correct
		* believability = likelihood different users accept the information
		* dissemination pathways = how content spreads within broader socio-technical systems
* evaluation infrastructure = shared tools, datasets, and standards provided by functioning institutions
	* enables thorough, consistent assessment regimes across organisations
