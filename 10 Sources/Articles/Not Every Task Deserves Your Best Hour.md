# Not Every Task Deserves Your Best Hour

<div align="center">
  <img src="https://pbs.twimg.com/profile_images/2073558169029124096/9WunitUs.jpg" width="220" />
</div>

- Three hours of work that should've taken one is a brutal waste of time. And you don't have to be slow, lazy, or distracted for it to happen.
    - Some work takes twice as long when you give it the wrong version of yourself.
- A few messages. Some admin. A small problem that was waiting. Maybe one or two decisions you already knew how to make.
    - An hour passes, and it feels productive because it was.
    - Then you get to the work that actually makes you think.
    - Now the sentence takes three attempts. You reread the same paragraph. The problem that normally opens up after ten minutes stays muddy.
    - You can still finish. It just takes much longer to get somewhere you would've reached with a clearer head.
- An hour isn't equally valuable for every kind of work.
    - Some work barely changes depending on when you do it. A routine email written later in the day may still be almost exactly the email you would've written earlier.
- You can use one of your best hours productively and still give it to the wrong work.
    - The work that needs your clearest thinking least is often the easiest to start.
    - The work that really benefits from a clear head often leaves more for you to figure out.
        - What actually matters here?
        - Where is the real problem?
        - What would make this good instead of merely finished?
- Something being ready for you doesn't mean it deserves the best version of you.

## Not every important task deserves your best hour

- Doing the hardest thing first isn't always the answer. Neither is blindly doing whatever seems most important.
    - The better question is: **Would this come out almost as well if I did it later, when I'm less sharp?**
    - With a clear head, you can hold more of the problem at once. You notice the connection sooner. You catch the mistake before it costs you another twenty minutes.
- And worse thinking doesn't always show up as worse work. Sometimes you refuse to lower the standard, so the cost shows up in time instead.
    - You rewrite. Recheck. Circle around the same problem. Fix things you would've caught earlier.
    - The work still gets good. It just takes much longer to get there.
- When does difficult work move more cleanly? When do you understand faster, get stuck less, and hold a hard problem in your head without constantly having to find your way back into it?
    - That's the window worth protecting.
    - Not because the clock makes it special. **But because that's when difficult work takes less time to do well.**

## Protect the part of the day that's actually yours

- If that hour keeps disappearing into errands, messages, and little things you could've handled later, work didn't take it from you. You just never protected it.
    - The window doesn't have to be large to matter.
    - **You don't need control over the whole day to stop wasting the best part you do control.**
- Sometimes that means moving routine work later. Sometimes it means deciding what deserves the window before the window arrives. And sometimes waking earlier can help, but only if sleep moves with it.
- So protect the strongest recurring window you can realistically use.
    - Give it first to the work that becomes noticeably worse or slower when you're less sharp.
    - Let the work that survives a flatter version of you wait when it reasonably can.
    - Then the difficult hour stops becoming three. You reach the same standard faster.
