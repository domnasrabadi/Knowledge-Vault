# My biggest takeaways from @JoshWoodward (Head of @Google Labs, the...

<div align="center">
  <img src="https://pbs.twimg.com/profile_images/1592990461517389824/iln8hi1f.jpg" width="220" />
</div>

- 1. The best ideas almost never come from a design sprint or brainstorm.
- all started with small groups of people who were obsessed with an insight and couldn’t stop themselves from building. These ideas surfaced in the shower, over a weekend, in a hallway. The implication for teams everywhere is that the environment matters more than the process. “You cannot microwave a good idea.”
- **2. Fall in love with the problem, not the product.** Most ideas require three, four, or five significant pivots before they either find traction or get shut down.
- Teams that are attached to the problem—the underlying pain point or the user behavior they are trying to shift—can keep adjusting until they get it right.
- **4. Keep a list of what is “almost possible,” and move fast when something crosses over.** Google Labs keeps a live list of experiences that are almost possible.
- 5. Every company needs to think like a labs team now.
- every company needs to be spending time playing with the latest models, running far-out experiments, and being first to discover what they unlock before the competition does
- 7. Someone’s “unlearning rate” is one of the most valuable skills right now.
- How quickly can someone take an insight, validate or invalidate it through testing, and then let it go?
- 10. Google Labs manages the marathon-sprint tension through seasons.
- After Google I/O each year, many labs teams enter a mode that looks unproductive from the outside: no shipping, just hacking, building, and discovering.
